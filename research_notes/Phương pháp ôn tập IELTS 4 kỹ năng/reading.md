# IELTS Academic Reading: question types, legal passage sources, skill training and review methods (for extending the FlowEnglish reading bank)

Scope note: research done 2026-11-04 with about 17 search/fetch calls. Two primary PDFs (the Vietnamese learner study on IISTE and the ielts.org 2023 sample-tasks PDF) could not be text-extracted, so their details come from search snippets and are marked that way.

## 1. Question types, how often they appear, and which ones Vietnamese learners find hardest

### Takeaway
Officially there are 11 task families (Cambridge/ielts.org). Prep sites split these into about 14 types. Across Cambridge tests, TFNG/YNNG, the completion types (summary/sentence/note) and matching information come up most and in the largest blocks. Matching headings appears in most tests but in smaller blocks, and the mix changes a lot from test to test. Vietnamese learners say TFNG/YNNG is the hardest type (False and Not Given get confused), and that vocabulary gaps and time pressure are their biggest problems overall.

### Cited Findings
**Format (official)**
- Academic Reading has 3 sections and 2,150–2,750 words in total, taken from books, journals, magazines, newspapers and online sources. Texts are written for non-specialists, may be narrative, descriptive or discursive/argumentative, and may include diagrams or graphs. The test is 60 minutes, has 40 questions worth 1 mark each, and is scored in bands 1–9 — [Cambridge English test format](https://www.cambridgeenglish.org/exams-and-tests/ielts/test-format/)
- The official task families and the skill each tests are: Multiple choice (detail or gist); Identifying information, TFNG (find specific information or a contradiction); Identifying writer's views, YNNG (opinion or claim); Matching information (scan for a detail, example or explanation in a paragraph); Matching headings (main idea of a paragraph); Matching features (relationships between facts, e.g. researcher to finding); Matching sentence endings (main ideas); Sentence completion (detail, with a word limit); Summary/note/table/flow-chart completion (detail and main idea, words from the text or from a list); Diagram label completion (link text to a visual); Short-answer questions (factual detail, with a word limit) — [Cambridge English test format](https://www.cambridgeenglish.org/exams-and-tests/ielts/test-format/)
- IELTS Liz counts 14 types: matching headings, TFNG/YNNG, matching paragraph information, summary completion, sentence completion, multiple choice, list selection, choosing a title, classification, matching sentence endings, table completion, flow chart completion, diagram completion, short answer. She says the most common are matching headings, sentence completion, TFNG, matching paragraph information and multiple choice — [IELTS Liz: Reading Question Types](https://ieltsliz.com/ielts-reading-question-types/)
- **Answer order matters for UI and tips.** Answers come in passage order for TFNG, sentence completion, summary completion, MCQ, list selection and short answer. They do NOT come in order for matching headings, matching paragraph information, classification and matching sentence endings — [IELTS Liz](https://ieltsliz.com/ielts-reading-question-types/)
- IELTS decides which types appear and how many in each passage, and this changes from test to test. "You can't prepare by focusing on only a few types" — [IELTS Liz](https://ieltsliz.com/ielts-reading-question-types/) (as summarised in search results)
- Frequency claim: one site says matching headings appears in "70% of Academic tests" and rates it high difficulty ("save for last, takes the most time"). It calls TFNG "high frequency" and tricky (Not Given trips people up), sentence completion and MCQ "common", and matching information and summary completion "medium". The basis is 32 tests from Cambridge 18–21. This is a commercial blog and the method is not published — [IELTSBeacon](https://ieltsbeacon.com/ielts-answers/reading/)
- Breakdowns of single tests show how much the mix varies. C17 T3: summary completion 14, TFNG 8, matching info 7, MCQ 5. C20 T1: matching info 15, sentence/note completion 7, TFNG 6, MCQ 4. C14 T2: TFNG 11, sentence completion 8, summary 8, headings 8, matching info 5. C10 T4: matching info 13, sentence/note completion 11 — [english-allinone C17T3](https://english-allinone.com/ielts-reading/cambridge-17-test-3/), [C20T1](https://english-allinone.com/ielts-reading/cambridge-20-test-1/), [C14T2](https://english-allinone.com/ielts-reading/cambridge-14-test-2/), [C10T4](https://english-allinone.com/ielts-reading/cambridge-10-test-4/) (some breakdowns add up to fewer than 40, so the categories are incomplete)

**Vietnamese learners**
- In a 2023 study of Vietnamese university English majors (Journal of Education and Practice, IISTE), most students named TFNG/YNNG as the most challenging task type, with trouble telling Not Given apart from False. Lack of vocabulary and time pressure were the two most noticeable difficulties overall. A survey item covered mental translation into Vietnamese while reading. The authors note that almost no research exists on how Vietnamese learners develop IELTS reading strategies — [ResearchGate: IELTS Reading: Perceived Challenges and Strategies by Vietnamese University English Majors](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/375602343_IELTS_Reading_Perceived_Challenges_and_Strategies_by_Vietnamese_University_English_Majors); [IISTE PDF](https://iiste.org/Journals/index.php/JEP/article/download/61676/63664) (from search snippets; authors and sample size not retrieved)
- Related literature lists three difficulty sources: complex sentence structure and vocabulary, the pressure of 40 questions in 60 minutes, and confusion from question types such as TFNG — same ResearchGate record (snippet)
- In a general (non-IELTS) study, most of 50 Vietnamese first-year non-English-major EFL students had vocabulary difficulties in reading comprehension (Van, 2021, as cited) — [ResearchGate: Common Difficulties of Reading Comprehension Experienced by Vietnamese Students](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/355268189_Common_Difficulties_of_Reading_Comprehension_Experienced_by_Vietnamese_Students)
- A Vietnamese prep blog calls TFNG/YNNG a type Vietnamese students "often get trouble with" (anecdotal) — [Prosource Vietnam](https://prosourceblog.wordpress.com/2013/10/28/prosource-vietnam-ielts-reading-strategies-part-1/). IELTS Advantage calls TFNG "probably the most difficult question on the reading paper" (not specific to Vietnam) — [IELTS Advantage](https://www.ieltsadvantage.com/2015/04/27/ielts-reading-true-false-not-given-tips/)
- Why Not Given is hard: False/No means the passage contradicts the statement, while Not Given means there is not enough information (Simon, via secondhand notes) — [Scribd copy of ielts-simon reading notes](https://www.scribd.com/document/454874640/kupdf-net-ielts-simoncom-ielts-reading-pdf). A common trap is answering from your own world knowledge when the text does not state the fact — [Worddemy TFNG paraphrase method](https://worddemy.com/ielts-reading/true-false-not-given-paraphrase-recognition/)

### Inferences
- The bank should be balanced by **question-type quota**, not by passage count. A sensible target is roughly 25–30% TFNG/YNNG, 25–30% completion (summary/sentence/note/table/flow chart), 15–20% matching information, about 10% headings, and the rest MCQ, matching features, matching endings, short answer and diagram. Treat this as a judgement from the sampled breakdowns, not an official ratio.
- Tag every question with `type`, `in_order: bool`, `answer_source: passage|list` (completion), and `word_limit`. These drive both the tips and the drill filters.
- Vietnamese-specific priorities: (1) a TFNG/YNNG "False vs Not Given" discrimination drill, (2) vocabulary/paraphrase support, (3) timed pacing. Low-value types such as "choosing a title" and "list selection" are rare and need only a few items.

### Gaps
- I found no official or peer-reviewed frequency statistics for each question type. The figures above come from blogs.
- I found no Vietnam-specific data on matching headings or matching information. Only TFNG has learner-survey evidence. The sample size and authors of the IISTE study were not retrieved (the PDF could not be parsed).

## 2. Legal sources of passages and best practice for AI-written IELTS-style passages

### Takeaway
Cambridge books and official IELTS samples are copyrighted, and IDP says the official sample texts are "authentic", taken from third-party publications. Link to them; do not copy them. The safe pool for reuse is: VOA/VOA Learning English (public domain, except wire content), US federal government works, UK OGL v3.0 content (commercial use OK with attribution), Project Gutenberg texts (strip the PG trademark), and Wikipedia (CC BY-SA 4.0, where ShareAlike applies to adaptations). AI-written original passages are workable, but item quality needs human or automated review.

### Cited Findings
**Official samples (use as links or a reference shelf, not as copied content)**
- ielts.org hosts official Academic sample task pages and PDFs with answer keys: multiple choice, note completion, table completion, matching features, summary completion, sentence completion, matching sentence endings, TFNG. There is also a combined 46-page 2023 PDF — [ielts.org sample questions](https://www.ielts.org/take-a-test/preparation-resources/sample-test-questions/academic-test); [Academic Reading sample tasks 2023 PDF](https://ielts.org/cdn/Sample-tests/ielts-academic-reading-sample-tasks-2023.pdf)
- British Council has free official Academic Reading sample tasks with answer keys and a computer-delivered practice test (not timed) — [takeielts.britishcouncil.org reading](https://takeielts.britishcouncil.org/take-ielts/prepare/free-ielts-english-practice-tests/reading)
- IDP has free sample questions and says the texts are authentic and come from books, journals, magazines and newspapers, so they include third-party copyright — [IDP free practice tests](https://ielts.idp.com/prepare/article-free-practice-tests)
- I found no reuse licence for these official samples (no terms surfaced in search). Treat them as all rights reserved.

**Open or reusable sources**
- **VOA**: "All text, audio and video material produced exclusively by the Voice of America is in the public domain". Credit VOA. Third-party material (AP, Reuters, AFP and so on) on VOA is NOT public domain — [VOA Terms of Use](https://learningenglish.voanews.com/p/6021.html). VOA Learning English texts, MP3s, photos and videos may be reprinted for educational and commercial purposes with credit to learningenglish.voanews.com. Wire-agency stories and photos are excluded — [VOA Learning English: Request Our Content](https://learningenglish.voanews.com/p/6861.html); Wikimedia Commons also warns to check credit lines on VOA images — [Commons: VOA logo file page](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Voice_of_America_Logo.svg)
- **US federal works**: under US copyright law, a work made by a federal employee as part of official duties is public domain. This is the basis for VOA's status — [VOA Terms](https://learningenglish.voanews.com/p/6021.html); see also [Wikinews: Public domain news sources](https://en.wikinews.org/wiki/Wikinews:Public_domain_news_sources)
- **UK OGL v3.0**: you may copy, publish, adapt and "exploit the Information commercially and non-commercially", including in apps. You must acknowledge the source with the provider's attribution statement and link the licence where possible. Third-party rights, trademarks and personal data are excluded. Check that a document is really under the OGL and not the non-commercial or charged licence. The OGL is compatible with CC BY — [National Archives OGL user guidance](https://cdn.nationalarchives.gov.uk/documents/information-management/ogl-user-guidance.pdf); [OGL v3.0 text](https://www.nationalarchives.gov.uk/doc/open-government-licence/version/3/); [National Archives attribution statements](https://www.nationalarchives.gov.uk/information-management/re-using-public-sector-information/uk-government-licensing-framework/open-government-licence/copyright-notices-attribution-statements/); [Wikipedia: Open Government Licence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Open_Government_Licence)
- **Project Gutenberg**: most PG ebooks are public domain in the US and can be used "as you please", including commercially and for derivative works. "Project Gutenberg" is a registered trademark, so remove the PG header, footer, licence and name to avoid trademark terms. Some PG books are copyrighted works posted with permission, and outside the US you must check local law — [PG License](https://www.gutenberg.org/policy/license.html); [PG Permission How-to](https://www.gutenberg.org/policy/permission.html); [PGLAF Cease and Desist page](https://cand.pglaf.org/). PG texts are mostly pre-1930 literature, so they suit extensive reading or style more than modern Academic topics (inference).
- **Wikipedia**: text is under CC BY-SA 4.0 (and GFDL). Attribution is a hyperlink to the page, an alternate URL, or an author list, plus a licence notice. **Changes you make must be released under CC BY-SA**. Images are licensed separately — [Wikipedia: Reusing Wikipedia content](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Reusing_Wikipedia_content)
- **CC BY-SA 4.0 deed**: commercial use is allowed. You must give credit, link the licence and indicate changes. Adaptations must be distributed under the same or a compatible licence — [CC BY-SA 4.0 deed](https://creativecommons.org/licenses/by-sa/4.0/)
- A Georgetown library guide lists open-access ESL/EFL resources (a starting directory) — [Georgetown: Open Resources ESL/EFL](https://library.georgetown.edu/scholarly-communication/open-access-esl-efl)

**AI-generated passages and items: evidence**
- Säuberli & Clematide (2024): zero-shot GPT-4 and Llama 2 reading comprehension items were mostly rated acceptable by humans. Llama 2 was worst, with 11 of 180 items "unusable". They propose an evaluation protocol that includes "text informativity", meaning whether an item can be answered without the text — [ACL Anthology 2024.readi-1.3](https://aclanthology.org/2024.readi-1.3/)
- Sayin & Gierl (2024): 12,500 GPT-3.5-assisted items were expert-screened (Accept/Minor/Major/Reject) and then field-tested on 1,607 students. They showed good difficulty and discrimination, and the distractors worked — [EM:IP](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/emip.12590)
- Rashid et al. (2024): ChatGPT-3.5 passages were read faster, understood better and rated higher in quality than human ones. But the AI struggled to write complex questions and answer choices — [HFES 2024 PDF](https://thereadabilityconsortium.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/09/rashid-et-al-2024-humanizing-ai-in-education-a-readability-comparison-of-llm-and-human-created-educational-content.pdf)
- BEA 2025: GPT-4o inference questions were 93.8% good quality, but only 42.6% matched the targeted inference type, so type fidelity is the weak point — [ACL Anthology BEA 2025](https://aclanthology.org/2025.bea-1.31.pdf)
- A 2026 arXiv paper reports that LLMs struggle to measure item discrimination between proficiency levels — [arXiv 2606.18709](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2606.18709); related: [LLM-critique item acceptance prediction](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2608.06609), [AI vs human passages for large-scale assessment (2025)](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40536-025-00255-w)

### Inferences
- **Recommended content pipeline:** (a) original AI passages as the main source. (b) "Seed" factual content from OGL, US federal or VOA sources, used as a fact base for AI rewriting, stored with a `source_url` and `licence` field. (c) Avoid Wikipedia-derived passages unless you are willing to publish those passages under CC BY-SA. ShareAlike applies to the adaptation, so mark them clearly and add a licence footer. (d) Link out to official ielts.org/BC samples; do not ingest them.
- **AI passage spec** (to match the official format): 700–950 words per passage (2,150–2,750 words over 3), non-specialist academic register, 6–8 lettered paragraphs (needed for headings and matching info), at least one hedged writer-opinion section (needed for YNNG), at least one process or chronology (flow chart), named researchers or dates (matching features, scanning). Increase difficulty from P1 to P3.
- **AI item QA checklist** (from the research above): (1) a "text-free answerability" check, where an LLM answers without the passage and the item is rejected if it gets it right; (2) a type-fidelity check, e.g. every NG item must have no supporting or contradicting sentence, and every F item must have an explicit contradicting sentence with its location stored; (3) paraphrase distance, so questions do not reuse the exact wording of the passage; (4) a word-limit check for completion answers; (5) an admin Accept/Minor/Major/Reject review state, as in Sayin & Gierl; (6) later, track p-value and discrimination per item from real user responses and retire bad items.
- Store `evidence_span` (paragraph letter plus character offsets) for every answer at generation time. This powers the "why wrong" explanations in section 4.

### Gaps
- I could not confirm reuse terms for ielts.org/BC/IDP samples (no terms page surfaced). I did not research Vietnamese copyright-law exceptions for education. Get legal review if you plan any commercial use of third-party text.
- I did not verify NASA, NIH, NOAA or other agency-specific policies one by one (US federal works are generally public domain, but agencies sometimes host third-party material).

## 3. Skill training beyond full tests

### Takeaway
The useful non-test drills are: keyword-to-paraphrase tables, which IELTS Simon makes central; "False vs Not Given" discrimination; paragraph main-idea and heading drills; scan-only drills limited to names, numbers and dates; and timed fluency reading on easy text, with WPM charted toward about 250 wpm. Fluency work should use text with 98% or more known vocabulary. Nation's framework gives about a quarter of course time to fluency.

### Cited Findings
- **Paraphrase / keyword technique (IELTS Simon):** underline keywords in the question, then look for paraphrases rather than identical words in the passage. Scanning works only for easy targets (names, numbers, years); for ordinary words like "buy", the passage may say "purchase". Keep a two-column table, "keywords in questions" against "similar words in the passage", for every test — [secondhand notes of ielts-simon: Scribd](https://www.scribd.com/document/454874640/kupdf-net-ielts-simoncom-ielts-reading-pdf), [Zhihu notes](https://zhuanlan.zhihu.com/p/490648270)
- Question writers deliberately put exact word matches in sentences that do NOT answer the question, because candidates scan for identical words. Build synonym sets around common IELTS ideas (increase/decrease, cause/effect) — [ieltsforfree: Paraphrasing in IELTS Reading](https://www.ieltsforfree.com/ielts-reading-paraphrasing/); [TechIELTS keyword technique](https://techielts.com/step-by-step-guide-ielts-reading-keyword-technique/)
- Daily micro-drill: take one question, write 3–4 synonyms for each keyword, then locate the answer. Review weekly which keyword transformations you missed — [Worddemy keyword transformation](https://worddemy.com/ielts-reading/keyword-transformation-technique/)
- **Reading speed:** Nation & Malarcher's *Reading for Speed and Fluency* targets 250 wpm as "the target reading speed for fluent second language reading". It uses passages of similar length and difficulty with little or no new vocabulary, MCQ comprehension checks and a speed progress chart — [AbeBooks listing / book description](https://www.abebooks.com/9781599661025/Reading-Speed-Fluency-Intermediate-Level-1599661020/plp); [Google Books](https://books.google.com/books/about/Reading_for_Speed_and_Fluency.html?id=U6R1MgAACAAJ)
- Chung & Nation: 40 Korean university students did 23 timed readings over 9 weeks, and the average rate rose from 141 to 214 wpm with no vocabulary added. Choose texts with at least 98% known words for speed practice. Nation's four-strands framework gives about 1/4 of time to fluency. Caveat: speed training reliably increases speed but not always comprehension in short studies — [bireader blog (secondary)](https://bireader.com/blog/reading-speed-the-skill-nobody-trains)
- Official skill mapping: matching headings tests main idea, matching information tests scanning for detail, and YNNG tests writer's views. This gives a direct "skill to drill" map — [Cambridge English test format](https://www.cambridgeenglish.org/exams-and-tests/ielts/test-format/)
- Vietnamese learners report translating in their heads while reading, a habit that slows reading and that fluency training targets — [ResearchGate Vietnamese study](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/375602343_IELTS_Reading_Perceived_Challenges_and_Strategies_by_Vietnamese_University_English_Majors) (snippet)

### Inferences (drill catalogue to add to FlowEnglish)
1. **Paraphrase Match** (2–3 min): show a question keyword phrase and 4 passage snippets, and the learner taps the paraphrase. Generate it from the stored `evidence_span` against the question stem. Track a "paraphrase recognition rate".
2. **F vs NG Sorter**: a statement plus one paragraph, answered T/F/NG, with feedback that highlights the contradicting sentence (F) or explains what is missing (NG). Build in a "world-knowledge trap" item class.
3. **Main-idea → Heading**: one paragraph at a time. First write or choose a 5-word gist, then pick a heading from distractors that share the paragraph's details but not its main idea. Progress to the full 6–8 paragraph headings set.
4. **Scan Sprint**: find a name, number or date in under 15 seconds. Timed, with a leaderboard of personal bests.
5. **Skim-30s**: read for 30–60 seconds, then answer "what is the passage mainly about / which paragraph discusses X".
6. **Sentence Simplifier**: take a long academic sentence (relative clauses, nominalisation, passive), have the learner choose the simplest equivalent meaning, then show the clause breakdown. This targets the "complex sentence structure" difficulty.
7. **Vocabulary-in-context**: a word with its sentence, choose the meaning, and add it to the SRS deck with the source sentence.
8. **Fluency Reader / WPM tracker**: 400–600-word easy passages (≥98% known words, graded by CEFR or band), timed, with 5–10 comprehension MCQs. Plot WPM over time, aim for 250 wpm, and show WPM only when comprehension is at least 70% (inference, matching Nation's approach of checking comprehension).
9. **Extensive reading shelf** graded A2–C1 from public-domain or OGL/VOA Learning English sources, untimed, with an optional 3-question check.
10. **Section-timed practice** (P1 17 min / P2 20 min / P3 23 min style pacing). This is my inference and not from an official source.

### Gaps
- I did not retrieve the primary Chung & Nation paper, so the numbers are from a secondary blog. I found no IELTS-specific study linking WPM to band score.
- I found no research on "sentence simplification" drills specifically for IELTS. The recommendation is inferred from the learner-difficulty findings.

## 4. Review methods: spaced repetition, error analysis, "why wrong" explanations

### Takeaway
Spacing has strong evidence. Cepeda et al. (2006) synthesised 839 assessments in 317 experiments. Spaced retrieval beats massed retrieval, and expanding and uniform schedules perform about the same, so a simple SRS is enough. Combine it with a per-question-type error log and evidence-located explanations, which prep sources recommend: weekly review of which paraphrase you missed.

### Cited Findings
- Cepeda, Pashler, Vul, Wixted & Rohrer (2006), *Psychological Bulletin* 132(3): 839 assessments of distributed practice in 317 experiments from 184 articles. The optimal gap between study sessions grows with the retention interval — [Cepeda 2006 PDF](https://www.evullab.org/pdf/CepedaPashlerVulWixtedRohrer-PB-2006.pdf); [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/7062225_Distributed_Practice_in_Verbal_Recall_Tasks_A_Review_and_Quantitative_Synthesis)
- Latimier, Peyre & Ramus (2021), *Educational Psychology Review*: from 39 comparisons, spaced retrieval practice gave a strong benefit over massed retrieval. Expanding and uniform schedules differed almost not at all (g = 0.034) — [Latimier et al. 2021 PDF](http://www.lscp.net/persons/ramus/docs/EPR20.pdf)
- Kim & Webb (2022), *Language Learning*: a meta-analysis of 48 experiments with 3,411 L2 learners found a medium-to-large spacing effect for L2 vocabulary, with equal and expanding schedules equally effective (g ≈ 1.15 on delayed tests, a figure from a secondary summary, not verified) — [memormore summary (secondary)](https://memormore.app/blogs/spaced-repetition-research)
- Prep-method guidance: review wrong answers to see which paraphrase caught you out, and log weekly which keyword changes you missed — [ieltsforfree](https://www.ieltsforfree.com/ielts-reading-paraphrasing/); [Worddemy](https://worddemy.com/ielts-reading/keyword-transformation-technique/). Write down key vocabulary from every test (Simon, secondhand) — [Scribd notes](https://www.scribd.com/document/454874640/kupdf-net-ielts-simoncom-ielts-reading-pdf)
- Competitors advertise "clear, expert explanations for every answer" as a selling point — [ielts.international](https://www.ielts.international/ielts-reading-practice-test)
- Third-party reviews say mini-ielts gives instant answer checking and a "View Solution" view, but have limits ("what they leave out") — [IELTSbiz: Mini IELTS review](https://ieltsbiz.com/blog/mini-ielts-reading-practice)

### Inferences (review features to add)
- **Missed-question SRS**: every wrong (or slow and correct) question goes into a queue. Re-show it at about 1d, 3d, 7d, 21d. Uniform-ish spacing is fine, because expanding schedules show no advantage. On re-show, ask the learner to locate the evidence first (tap the sentence), then answer. This is retrieval rather than re-reading.
- **Vocabulary SRS from reading**: cards carry the word plus the original passage sentence plus the paraphrase pair (question wording against passage wording). Paraphrase pairs make particularly good cards for IELTS.
- **Error taxonomy per wrong answer** (auto-suggested, user-confirmable): (a) could not locate the evidence; (b) located it but missed the paraphrase or synonym; (c) F/NG confusion; (d) word-limit or spelling error; (e) distractor trap (exact word match); (f) time ran out; (g) world-knowledge assumption. Show a dashboard of accuracy by question type × error cause, and recommend the matching drill from section 3.
- **"Why wrong" explanation template**: correct answer → evidence (paragraph letter, highlighted sentence, scroll-to) → paraphrase map (question words ↔ passage words) → why the learner's choice is wrong (e.g. "the passage says X; your choice requires Y which is never stated → NG") → a one-line tip for the type. Generate it from the stored `evidence_span`, and give it a Vietnamese gloss toggle.
- **Time-per-question telemetry** so slow correct answers can feed the review queue and the pacing advice.

### Gaps
- I found no study measuring SRS of *missed IELTS questions* specifically. The recommendation extrapolates from the spacing and retrieval literature.
- The Kim & Webb effect size was not checked against the original paper.

## 5. How major sites structure reading practice

### Takeaway
The market pattern has three layers: (1) learn by question type (lessons and tips), (2) practise by type or by part, single passages of about 13–14 questions filterable by topic and type, (3) full timed mocks in a computer-delivered style. The British Council has shifted its free offer to "IELTS Ready" with AI-marked mini mocks. Few sites offer fluency, paraphrase or SRS tools, so that is a gap FlowEnglish can fill.

### Cited Findings
- **ieltsonlinetests.com**: the Exam Library offers "Practice mode", where you choose a part or task (or the full test) and a time limit to improve accuracy and timing, and "Simulation test mode", which mimics computer-delivered IELTS — [IELTS Exam Library](https://ieltsonlinetests.com/ielts-exam-library?skill=reading&page=0); [reading practice test](https://ieltsonlinetests.com/reading-tips/ielts-reading-practice-test)
- **mini-ielts.com**: a free library of single-passage tests (about 13–14 mixed-type questions), grouped by topic (Recent Actual Tests, Science, Nature and environment and others) and filterable by question type (headings, matching info, MCQ, plan/map/diagram, sentence completion, summary/form completion). It has "Take Test" and "View Solution", needs no account, and allows unlimited retakes — [mini-ielts reading](https://mini-ielts.com/reading); [IELTSbiz review](https://ieltsbiz.com/blog/mini-ielts-reading-practice)
- **ielts-up.com**: 11 Academic and 2 General full tests of 40 questions in 3 sections, with answer checking and a score. Tips and vocabulary sit on separate pages — [ielts-up reading practice](https://ielts-up.com/reading/ielts-reading-practice.html)
- **IELTS Liz**: lessons organised by question type, each with a task description, required skills, tips and linked practice lessons — [IELTS Liz question types](https://ieltsliz.com/ielts-reading-question-types/); [TFNG practice lessons](https://ieltsliz.com/true-false-not-given-ielts-reading-practice/)
- **Road to IELTS (British Council / Clarity)**: organised by skill. Each skill has advice and tutorial videos, a "Practice zone" by question type and "Test practice" mocks. Tiers historically were Test Drive (free), Last Minute (about 20 h) and Full (120 h) — [About Road to IELTS (BC Egypt PDF)](https://www.britishcouncil.org.eg/sites/default/files/about_road_to_ielts_en.pdf); [roadtoielts.com](https://www.roadtoielts.com/). ### Inferences
- FlowEnglish already has full tests, practice passages, AI passages, per-type drills and lookup, which matches layers 1–3. The gaps against competitors are: (1) **mini-ielts-style topic and type filters** over a larger single-passage pool (aim for 40–60+ passages, each tagged by topic, type and difficulty band); (2) **ieltsonlinetests-style practice mode** (choose part or type, set a custom timer) next to strict simulation; (3) a **short "mini mock"** (one passage, 20 min) like BC's IELTS Ready; (4) features largely missing elsewhere: paraphrase drills, F/NG sorter, WPM fluency reader, missed-question SRS and an error-cause dashboard.
- Topic taxonomy for tagging: science/technology, environment/nature, history/archaeology, psychology/behaviour, education, health/medicine, business/economics, arts/culture, urban/transport, animals/biology. This is inferred from the mini-ielts categories and typical Cambridge topics. Spread new AI passages across these so the bank does not cluster.
